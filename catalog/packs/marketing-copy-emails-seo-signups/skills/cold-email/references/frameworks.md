# Frameworks for the body

Frameworks teach a way of thinking; do not paste them. Pick the one that fits what you know about the reader, then say it in your own plain words. The examples use an invented seller (a scheduling tool for dental clinics). Every figure inside the samples is an invented placeholder; replace it with a real, sourced fact of your own.

## Problem, agitate, solution (the default)

Name a pain, show what it costs, offer the fix, ask softly. Best when the reader feels the problem but has not shopped for a cure.

> Most clinic managers lose about four hours a week to no-show chasing. Over a year that is a full working month, and the empty chairs cost more than the hours. We send reminders that let patients rebook in one tap. Want to see how a clinic your size set it up?

## Before, after, bridge

Describe today, describe the better state, present your product as the way across. Best for offers with a clear transformation.

> Today your front desk phones every patient the day before. Picture those calls gone and the chairs still full. That is what our reminders do. Open to a quick look?

## Question, value, ask

A pointed question, one line of value, a direct next step. Best for busy senior readers.

> Is your front desk still phoning patients to confirm visits? We replace those calls with one-tap text reminders. Worth ten minutes?

## Attention, interest, desire, action

A striking fact, a link to their situation, proof, a call to act. Best for data-minded buyers.

> Clinics that confirm by text see far fewer empty slots. With your second site opening, that matters twice over. One group cut missed visits by a third. Fifteen minutes this week?

## Praise, picture, push

An honest compliment, a better picture, a gentle nudge. Needs a real trigger such as a talk or article.

> Your talk on running a small clinic well was clear about where time leaks. What if confirmations stopped taking any? Happy to show how. Open to a short chat?

## Customer story

Introduce a customer, their problem, the result. Use when you have a vivid case.

> Dana ran three chairs and answered phones between patients. After switching to text reminders her missed visits halved in two months. I think the same would work for you. Shall I send the details?

## Situation, complication, question

State what is true, what makes it hard, then ask.

> You added a second site this spring. That usually doubles the confirmation workload. How is your desk coping?

## Contrarian hook, benefit, proof, ask

Start with a claim that cuts against habit, explain, back it up. Best for analytical readers.

> Most clinics track how many reminders they send. The best track how many chairs fill. One clinic that switched filled six more slots a week with fewer messages. Worth a look?

## Compliment, case study, ask

Short and proof led. Strong for agencies and service firms.

> Great site. We built a booking flow for a clinic near you and have a few ideas for yours. Interested?

## Observation plus a yes or no question

One or two sentences. Maximum brevity, driven by curiosity.

> You are hiring a second receptionist. Would it help to automate the confirmation calls instead?

## Ultra-short trigger, hint, binary ask

One to three sentences, no introduction. Spend a short time on research (about a minute is a starting point) and use a segment-level or role-level signal; for top accounts, quote what they said in a public interview.

## Five-part base

Observation, insight, credibility, solution, invitation to talk. Reliable when nothing else fits.

## Long-form arc

Problem, amplify, story, testimony, offer, response. Spread the pieces over several touches in a sequence for complex services.
