# Price perception and behaviour design

## Price perception

- **Charm pricing (left-digit effect).** The first digit dominates, so 49 reads as far less than 50. Good for value-led products.
- **Round-price fluency.** Round numbers are easy to process and feel premium. A 600 euro consulting day signals quality; 597 signals a deal. Match the style to the positioning and keep it consistent across the page.
- **Rule of 100.** A rule of thumb popularised by the author Jonah Berger; evidence for the exact threshold is thin, so test it. Below 100 units of currency, a percentage looks bigger ("25% off a 60 euro item"). Above 100, a flat amount looks bigger ("80 euros off a 400 euro item"). Show whichever is larger-looking, but state the final price clearly.
- **Price relativity (good, better, best).** Buyers judge a price against its neighbours. Put the plan you most expect to fit in the middle, with a leaner plan below as a floor and a fuller plan above as a reference.
- **Mental accounting in prices.** The same cost feels different per day, per month or per year. Pick the real-unit framing that helps, such as "about 1 euro a day", and show the billed amount next to it.

## Design and delivery models

- **Hick's law.** More choices means slower decisions and more drop-off. One main call to action per screen, fewer form fields, a short menu.
- **AIDA.** Attention, interest, desire, action. Check that each section of a page or campaign serves the next stage and that action is not asked for before desire exists.
- **Repeated contact.** Buyers often need several contacts before acting; count the contacts in your own sales or signup data. Plan a sequence across channels (a post, a retargeting ad, an email series, a follow-up) rather than one big push.
- **Nudges and choice architecture.** Order, defaults, labels and friction guide choices without removing any. A nudge should be easy to resist and aligned with the buyer's interest.
- **Fogg behaviour model.** Action happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet. High desire with a hard task fails; an easy task with no prompt fails. Fix whichever of the three is missing.
- **EAST.** Make the action Easy, Attractive, Social and Timely: fewer steps, an appealing presentation, visible peers doing it, and an ask at a fitting moment.
- **COM-B.** Behaviour needs Capability, Opportunity and Motivation. Ask: can they do it, is the route open to them, do they want to? A gap in any one explains stalling.
- **Activation energy.** The effort to begin matters most. Pre-fill what you know, offer a template, and give a first win within a minute. Friction at the start is what loses people, even if the rest is easy.
- **North Star metric.** Choose one number that tracks delivered value, such as weekly active projects completed, and steer work toward it so teams do not optimise rival numbers.
- **Cobra effect.** Incentives can backfire. A referral reward may attract people who farm rewards rather than genuine fits. Pilot small, check quality of results, then scale.

Friction and conversion: when conversion is low, check Hick's law, activation energy and Fogg first; they are the cheapest to diagnose.
