# The seven passes

Do one pass at a time. In each pass, first mark problems without fixing, then propose specific edits, then check the edit keeps the author's meaning. After a pass, go back through the earlier ones.

## 1. Clarity

Question: can a stranger follow every sentence on first read?

Look for tangled sentence structure, pronouns with no clear referent, insider jargon, statements that can be read two ways, and missing context. The usual causes are a sentence that carries too much, abstract wording where a concrete noun would do, knowledge the reader does not have, and a main point buried under qualifiers.

Exit check: each section makes one main point, and the text speaks to the reader ("you") rather than about the company.

## 2. Voice and tone

Question: does it sound like one writer from start to finish?

Look for drifts between formal and casual, a personality that appears and disappears, a mood swing, and words that do not fit the brand. Typical slips: a casual opening that turns corporate by paragraph three, "we" mixed with "the company", humor in one block and gravity in the next by accident, a sudden technical term. Read the text aloud; the ear catches shifts the eye skips. Propose edits that smooth the joins and keep the personality.

Exit check: return to clarity, because voice edits often blur meaning.

## 3. So what

Question: does each claim tell the reader why they should care?

After every statement ask "so what?" If the answer is not on the page, add the bridge ("which means...") to a benefit the reader actually wants. Example: "Our app uses smart route planning" becomes "The app reorders your stops so the van drives fewer kilometres, which means the last delivery lands before the shop closes." (Illustration only; use the real facts of the product.) Weak spots: lists of features, impressive claims that lead nowhere, technical ability with no outcome, company milestones that do nothing for the reader.

Exit check: return to voice, then clarity.

## 4. Prove it

Question: is every claim backed by something the reader can see?

Flag unsupported assertions: "used by teams everywhere" (which ones?), "the fastest on the market" (measured by whom?), "people adore it" (show one person saying so). Kinds of proof to look for or request: named testimonials with detail, case studies, figures with a source, third-party validation, guarantees, customer logos, review scores. For each claim check whether proof sits nearby. If none exists, either ask for it or soften the claim. Never invent proof.

Exit check: return to so what, voice, clarity.

## 5. Specificity

Question: is it concrete enough that only this product could say it?

Hunt for vague verbs (improve, enhance, optimize), statements that fit any company, round numbers that look made up, and benefits with no size or timeframe. Swap abstraction for detail, for instance "work faster" to a stated saving per task, "a growing community" to the real member count, "friendly service" to the real pickup time. Use only facts the user gives you. If a line cannot be made specific, it is probably filler: cut it.

Exit check: return to prove it, so what, voice, clarity.

## 6. Heightened emotion

Question: does the reader feel anything?

Flat, informational text hides the pain it describes. Feelings worth considering: the sting of the current situation, annoyance with the alternatives, fear of missing out, wanting a different life or job, pride in a smart choice, relief once solved. Tools: describe the "before" vividly, use sensory words, tell a short story, name an experience the reader shares, ask a question that makes them reflect. Keep it honest: emotion supports the message and never pressures the reader.

Exit check: return to specificity, prove it, so what, voice, clarity.

## 7. Zero risk

Question: what could stop a reader from acting?

Work around each call to action. List every reason someone might hesitate: unanswered objections, missing trust signals, a next step that is unclear ("Get in touch" instead of saying what happens), fine print that raises doubt, costs that show up late. Risk reducers to check or suggest: money-back promise, free trial, no card needed, cancel anytime, proof placed next to the button, a plain description of what happens after the click, privacy reassurance. Only suggest ones the business really offers.

Exit check: one last pass back through emotion, specificity, prove it, so what, voice, clarity.
