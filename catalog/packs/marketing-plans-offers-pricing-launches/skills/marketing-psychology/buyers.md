# How buyers think and decide

Several of these effects replicate unevenly across studies, so read each as a hypothesis to test on your own audience, not a law. For each effect: what it is, then a use. Apply only in ways the buyer would accept if they saw exactly what you were doing.

- **Fundamental attribution error.** We blame people's character for what is often the situation. If visitors leave at payment, suspect the payment form before suspecting the visitors.
- **Mere exposure.** Familiar things feel safer. Show up in the same style, tone and colours across channels, week after week.
- **Availability heuristic.** What is easy to picture feels likely. A short story of a named customer's first week makes success easy to imagine.
- **Confirmation bias.** People hear what fits what they already believe. Start from their belief, then widen it. Attacking a held view head-on usually fails.
- **Lindy effect.** What has lasted tends to keep lasting. Clear value statements, reviews and fast support outlive fashionable tactics; do not drop them for a trend.
- **Mimetic desire.** We want what others want. A real waitlist, a real count of people on it, and visible users who resemble the buyer. Never invent the crowd.
- **Sunk cost fallacy.** Money already spent should not decide the next spend. Review a stalled campaign on its future return only.
- **Endowment effect.** Owning raises value. A trial, a sample or a free tier lets the buyer feel ownership; keep the paid step honest about what changes.
- **IKEA effect.** Effort raises attachment. A bike shop that lets a buyer pick frame colour and saddle will see the buyer value the result more.
- **Zero-price effect.** Free is a category of its own, not just a small price. Test it on your own price page rather than assuming the size of the pull. Free shipping and free tiers work, but state the true catch plainly.
- **Present bias.** Near rewards beat far ones. Lead with what the buyer gets this week ("the first invoice is sent in 5 minutes") before the yearly payoff.
- **Status-quo bias.** Staying put feels safe. Lower the cost of leaving the old tool: import, migration help, a stated rollback.
- **Default effect.** People keep the pre-chosen option. Defaults are fine when they serve the buyer (a sensible plan, privacy-protective settings). Pre-ticking a paid add-on or hiding an auto-renewal is a dark pattern; do not do it.
- **Paradox of choice.** Many options stall decisions. Offer a few tiers, not many (three is a starting point, not a measured value), and mark one as the best fit for most, with a reason.
- **Goal-gradient.** Effort speeds up near the finish. Show real progress ("3 of 4 steps done") in set-up flows and loyalty cards.
- **Peak-end rule.** People remember the best (or worst) moment and the last one. Plan one delightful moment and a warm ending: a thank-you page with a useful next step, a follow-up note that helps.
- **Zeigarnik effect.** The claim is that unfinished tasks stay in mind. A 2025 meta-analysis found the memory advantage hard to reproduce, while the urge to resume an interrupted task (the Ovsiankina effect) held up better. So use it as a reason to let people resume, such as a saved cart or a half-complete profile, and treat any lift as something to test. One reminder is help, ten is pressure.
- **Pratfall effect.** A small admitted flaw makes a capable brand more likable. "We do not do rush orders, but each one is hand-checked" earns trust.
- **Curse of knowledge.** Once you know it, you cannot unsee it. Have two people outside your field read the page and say what they think it sells.
- **Mental accounting.** People sort money into mental pots. "About 2 euros a day" sits in the coffee pot; "720 euros a year" sits in the budget pot. Pick the framing that is true and fair, and show the real total nearby.
- **Regret aversion.** Fear of a wrong choice blocks action. Refund windows, cancel-anytime terms and trials lower it. Make them real and easy to use.
- **Bandwagon and social proof.** Others' choices signal safety. Use real customer counts, dated reviews, recognisable logos you have permission to show, and honest "popular" labels. Never buy or fabricate them.
