## When to use

Use this when a person wants a video edited, tightened, restructured or reworked and the real question is what to keep, what to cut and what to show, not which button to press. Typical words: "make this better", "cut it down to two minutes", "add some b-roll", "turn this into something I can post", "it feels slow".

Work from the person's own file. If they give a link, ask whether the video is theirs or the platform allows downloading it; only then fetch it (a downloader such as yt-dlp is optional, never the default). Otherwise ask for the file.

Never build a video about a named private individual that depicts, imitates or targets them; decline and say why. Never clone or imitate a voice without its owner's consent.

Neighbours, where each takes over:
- `cut-spoken-recording`: the job is only removing silence, filler and slips from speech.
- `video-editor-bridge`: the work happens inside an editing program and needs its controls.
- `judge-media-by-measurement`: checking the finished result by numbers (loudness, length, frames).

This skill decides the edit. Those skills carry it out or check it.

## How it works

1. **Classify the scope first.** Pick one and say it to the person in a sentence:
   - Trim: change the start or end only.
   - Tighten: remove dead material, keep order and meaning.
   - Restructure: reorder, shorten hard, or build a new opening from later material.
   - Add visuals: layer pictures, text or graphics over what exists.
   - Full rework: all of the above, treated as directing the whole video.
   A narrow request stays narrow. A named sentence, a single caption or one time range gets the smallest complete change, plus a look at the neighbouring seconds so continuity holds. Do not turn a small ask into a rework.

2. **Look at the real material before judging it.** Get the length, the transcript or a description of what is said, and what the pictures actually show. Do not invent footage, reactions, results or facts that the material does not contain. If something needed is missing, say so and offer the options.

3. **Write the editorial thesis.** One sentence: what should the viewer feel or do at the end? For a full rework or restructure, tell the person the thesis and go ahead unless it would change what the speaker appears to say. Then test every cut, keep and addition against it. A moment that does not serve the thesis is a candidate for removal, however good it is.

4. **Keep meaning intact.** Preserve what the speaker meant, their qualifications, the chronology and their emotional intent. If the edit would make someone seem to say something they did not, stop and confirm first. Prefer edits that can be undone, and leave the original file untouched.

5. **Give every visual a job.** Allowed jobs: explain an idea, prove a claim, relieve a long static stretch, mark a change of topic or place. A visual with no job is removed. Stock or generated pictures must never look like proof of a real event, result, person or product. Do not put the same kind of card or graphic on every beat; vary weight so some moments land harder than others, and let the face carry moments where expression is the content.

6. **Use the matching playbook.**
   - Talking head: the words set the timing. Decide what to keep from the full text read as a whole, not sentence by sentence, because a line cut early may be the premise of a later one. Pictures can fill the opening and cover cuts, but they never decide which spoken sentences survive. Keep the original order unless the person agreed to reordering.
   - Montage: review the candidate clips once and sort into strong, usable and reject. Open on the strongest first shot. Order the rest by how well action and setting flow. Take a short piece from a long clip rather than burning the whole clip.
   - Audio-led (music or narration drives the cut): fix the audio first, then cut pictures to it. Music starts with the picture and ends exactly where the last visual ends, with a fade of a second or two in and two or three out. Under speech, sit the bed clearly below the voice; adjust in steps of about 3 dB. Raise it only in passages with no speech. No sung lyrics under talking, and match mood to content.

7. **Reject these faults even if each piece is technically fine:**
   - Jump cuts with no reason (a cut should hide a flaw or move the story).
   - On-screen text that disappears before it can be read at normal speed.
   - Music competing with speech.
   - Repeated b-roll, or footage that is merely nearby in topic.
   - Pictures or captions that all say the same sentence at once.
   - Effects placed at fixed intervals instead of for a reason.
   - Colours, fonts or motion changing from scene to scene with no intent.
   - Overlays used to hide a continuity problem that should be fixed by the edit, framing or sound.
   - An ending that trails off. Finish on the payoff and hold the last frame briefly.
   - A checklist video: captions, music and b-roll all present, but no argument and no progression.

8. **Place in one batch, then stop.** Put only the reviewed strong or usable ranges into the timeline in a single pass. Do not review again and re-place in a loop. After placing, do one honest check (see below) and, if something fails, fix the specific fault; if problems remain after three passes, report them instead of continuing.

9. **Ask only when the answer changes the deliverable.** Do not ask the person to confirm things you can read from the material, a fact you have verified, or a plan they have already approved. Ask when a choice changes the result and has no sensible default (for example, a shortening that drops nuance, or which of two takes to keep when both work). Ask one question at a time, and offer your recommended default.

10. **State paid costs first.** Before any generation, speech or music service that costs credits or money, tell the person what will be spent and wait for a yes. Use the service they already have set up; do not pick one for them. Free local work needs no such step.

11. **Report honestly.** End with what you changed, which numbers you measured (length, number of cuts), what you looked at (frames before and after each cut, first and last seconds), and what only a human ear or eye can judge (flow of cuts, tone of voice, music level).

## Checks before done

- The scope was named and the edit stayed within it.
- The thesis is written down and you can point to the reason each major cut or addition serves it.
- Every visual has a stated job; none pretends to prove a real claim.
- You viewed frames around each cut and at the start and end, and checked for black, frozen or stretched frames.
- Text on screen stays long enough to read, and music sits under speech and ends with the picture.
- None of the listed faults is present, or any remaining one is reported to the person.
- The original file is unchanged and the result was saved as a new file.
- Any paid step was approved by the person after its cost was stated.
- The final message separates measured, looked at, and needs a human to judge.
