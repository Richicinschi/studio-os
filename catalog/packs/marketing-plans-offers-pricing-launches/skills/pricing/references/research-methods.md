# Price and packaging research

Numbers in samples are invented. Counts such as sample sizes and cut-offs are starting points, not measured values.

## Van Westendorp price sensitivity

Ask each respondent four questions about the product:
1. At what price is it so expensive you would not consider it?
2. At what price is it so cheap you would doubt its quality?
3. At what price is it getting expensive, but you might still buy?
4. At what price is it a bargain?

Analysis: plot a cumulative curve per question and read four crossings. By convention the "too cheap" and "bargain" curves are drawn inverted, which is what lets the four lines cross.
- Marginal cheapness: "too cheap" meets "expensive".
- Marginal expensiveness: "too expensive" meets "bargain".
- Optimal point: "too cheap" meets "too expensive".
- Indifference point: "expensive" meets "bargain".

The acceptable range runs from marginal cheapness to marginal expensiveness; the sweet zone lies between the optimal and indifference points.

Tips: collect 100-300 responses, split by persona because willingness to pay differs, describe the product realistically, and add a purchase-intent question.

Sample reading (invented): marginal cheapness 8, optimal 14, indifference 17, marginal expensiveness 24 per month. Sweet zone 14-17. Current price 11 sits below it, so a rise is worth testing.

## MaxDiff (best-worst scaling)

1. List 8-15 candidate features.
2. Show sets of 4-5 at a time.
3. For each set, respondents pick the most and least important.
4. Repeat over enough sets that every feature is compared several times.
5. Statistics turn the picks into utility scores.

Use the ranking for packaging:
- Top 20%: in every plan, they are table stakes.
- 20-50%: use these to separate plans.
- 50-80%: higher plans only.
- Bottom 20%: cut, or sell as an add-on.

## Willingness-to-pay questions

- Direct ("what would you pay?"): easy, but biased.
- Gabor-Granger: ask whether they would buy at a price, step through a series of prices (the order differs between practitioners), and chart the share saying yes at each price as a demand curve. Stated intent tends to overstate real buying.
- Conjoint: show bundles at different prices, let people choose, and estimate price sensitivity per feature.

## Usage-to-value analysis (existing customers)

1. Record usage: feature frequency, volume (users, records, calls), outcomes (time saved, revenue made).
2. Compare with success: which patterns predict staying, which predict growing, which accounts pay most.
3. Find thresholds: where customers "get it", where they expand, where the price should step up.

Sample finding (invented): accounts that stayed over a year averaged 9 active users and 3 integrations; churned accounts averaged 2 users and none. Value tracks team adoption and depth, so charge per user and gate integrations to upper plans.
