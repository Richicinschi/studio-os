# Measuring results and method families

This file holds no industry averages. Cold-email results swing with the list, the offer and the sender's reputation, and no public study is cited here, so build your own baseline and use the levers below as things to test, not targets to hit.

## Measure your own baseline

| Metric | What to count | Compare with |
|---|---|---|
| Bounce rate | bounced mails divided by mails sent | your own previous campaign; stop and clean the list if it climbs |
| Reply rate | replies divided by mails delivered | the previous campaign, or a control half of the same list |
| Positive reply share | interested replies divided by all replies | the same split |
| Meeting rate | meetings booked divided by mails delivered | the same split |
| Open rate | opens divided by mails delivered, only as a rough signal because tracking pixels distort it | prefer replies and meetings |

Count over a full sequence, not one day, and write down the denominator each time. To test a change, split one list at random into two halves, change one thing for one half, and compare after the full sequence has run. Keep the complaint and unsubscribe counts next to the reply counts.

## A worked funnel

All numbers below are invented for illustration, not a benchmark. Send 1,000 emails. Suppose 300 are opened. Suppose 60 reply. If a quarter of replies are positive that is 15, and if 40% of those become meetings that is 6. If one meeting in five closes, that is about 1 customer, roughly 0.1% end to end. Replace each invented rate with your own measured one and recompute; the point is to see which stage is weakest.

## Levers worth testing, one at a time

1. Hook type. Test a timing-based hook (something just happened to them) against a generic problem hook.
2. Personalization depth. Test a detail tied to the problem against merge fields only.
3. Brevity. Test a shorter body against a longer one.
4. Narrow targeting. Test small, tightly chosen lists against broad ones.
5. A first follow-up. Compare replies before and after it is sent.
6. Plain reading level. Test simpler wording against denser wording.
7. Send day and time. Test a few weekdays in the reader's time zone.

## Why results can fall over time

Inboxes are crowded, mailbox providers keep tightening their spam rules, and machine-written volume has grown. Keep spam complaints low and read the mailbox providers' published sender requirements for the current complaint threshold. The gap between careless and careful senders tends to widen, so craft matters more.

## Split by seniority and industry

Track your results separately for each seniority level and each industry you mail. Small groups give noisy numbers, so wait for enough sends before drawing a conclusion, and let your own data decide where to spend effort.

## Fifteen common mistakes

1. Too long, and written at a harder reading level than the reader wants.
2. Too much about the sender; count the I/we sentences.
3. No clear value; decision makers skip what looks irrelevant.
4. Generic template with a name swapped in.
5. A feature list instead of one proof point.
6. Fake personalization ("loved your post" with no detail).
7. Asking for a long call in the first touch.
8. Pushy words stacked together, which invites spam flagging.
9. No call to action.
10. Follow-ups that say you never heard back.
11. Tone wrong for the role.
12. Buzzwords instead of plain statements.
13. Big claims without proof.
14. Mailing too many people at one company.
15. Fake urgency or faked reply and forward prefixes.

## Regional tone

| | US | UK | Germany and neighbours | Nordics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tone | direct, casual | polite, professional | precise, data first | factual, equal footing |
| Length | short, blunt | longer, insight led | detailed | concise but substantive |
| Proof | outcome numbers | research-backed | technical accuracy | shared values |

These are tendencies to check against your own replies, not rules. Sequences can be shorter and more direct in the US, and carry more personality in the UK. European data-protection law shapes both tone and what you may send; see the legal note in the main skill.

## Method families seen in practice

- Compliment, then a case study, then an ask. Suits agencies and high-ticket services.
- Neutral question that makes a hidden problem visible. Suits consultative selling.
- Research-heavy personalization, scaled with tooling. Bridges mass and deep.
- Personality and values based openers, grouped into reusable premises.
- Coaching-style frameworks built from reply data.
- One to three sentence mails that quote the prospect's own words. Suits high-volume teams.
- Same-day multi-channel opening (email, social, call) with tiered personalization.
