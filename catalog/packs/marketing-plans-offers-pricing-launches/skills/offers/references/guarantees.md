# Designing a guarantee

A guarantee raises belief (the buyer reasons "they would not offer this unless they were sure") and lowers the emotional risk of buying. A badly chosen one costs more than none, so match the type to your model.

## Eight types

| Type | Promise | Good fit | Backfires when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unconditional refund | Money back within N days, no questions | Cheap products, trusting audience | Price is high or delivery is hands-on |
| Conditional refund | Refund if you did X and still lack Y | Programs that need effort | Buyers are expert; hard to honor in public |
| Refund plus extra | Refund and a payment on top | Strong margin, reliable delivery | Any failure becomes a public story |
| Service level | Late or missing delivery means money back | Fixed-scope services | The measure is vague |
| Pay on performance | You pay only when a result occurs | Expert B2B, confident delivery | Long cycles, murky attribution |
| No-refund stance | Final sale, decide carefully | Premium, screened buyers | First-time or nervous buyers |
| Result or extension | No result by date means we keep working free | Coaching, extendable work | Open-ended cost |
| Comparison | Beat a named alternative or refund | A fair comparison exists | The rival cannot be measured |

## Choosing

1. Name the buyer's biggest fear.
   - "It might not work": refund family.
   - "You might deliver late": service level.
   - "I pay and get nothing": pay on performance or result-or-extension.
   - "This might be a scam": comparison, or proof and specifics.
2. Know your refund tolerance. Can you absorb many refunds? Then unconditional. Need to filter? Conditional. Cannot afford any? No-refund stance backed by strong proof.
3. Consider sophistication. Premium repeat buyers read "no refunds" as confidence. Newcomers need permission to try. Corporate buyers expect commercial terms such as service levels.
4. Check measurability. A clean metric allows performance, comparison or extension types. A fuzzy result calls for refund-with-a-gate, where the gate is work completed.

## Examples by business

- Course: "Finish all modules inside 60 days and submit the final task; if you still have no [named result], full refund." Weak: a bare "money back".
- Coaching: "After two sessions, if it does not look right, we stop and refund what is unused." Weak: "satisfaction guaranteed".
- Fixed-scope service: "Month one is a paid pilot; if the agreed milestone is missed you skip month two." Weak: "we work until you are happy".
- Premium community: "No refunds; applications are screened so you only join if it fits." Weak: a generic 30-day refund, which looks cheap there.
- Cheap download: "30 days, no questions." Weak: nothing at all.
- Software: free trial, or annual plan with a 30-day refund. Weak: "cancel anytime" alone, which is just normal terms.
- Cold paid traffic: a stronger-than-refund promise, because that audience expects it. Weak: the same plain refund every ad offers.

## Wording

Works: specific terms ("if after the first three weeks you cannot point to one result, email us for a full refund"), a confident tone, and admitting that guarantees can sound slick before giving a plain one.

Does not work: "100% satisfaction", "lifetime guarantee" with no conditions, several guarantees stacked, and legal-sounding walls of text.

## Frequent mistakes

- Promising more than you can deliver. Stress test: what if one buyer in ten claims it?
- Too many conditions. Keep to two; three only if they are closely tied.
- Burying it in fine print. If it is your strongest belief lever, put it large near the buy button.
- Never re-reading it. Review the wording twice a year against the current offer.
- Using it to replace proof. Proof first, then the guarantee.

## When no guarantee is right

A no-refund stance suits expert buyers, screened applicants, premium prices, established brands, and overwhelming proof. The message is "the work is real and you have qualified yourself". To the wrong audience it reads cold.

## Quick audit

1. State the buyer's real risk in one concrete sentence.
2. Pick the type that reverses that exact risk.
3. Multiply expected refund rate by refund cost and confirm you can carry it.
4. Check the fit with the audience.

Most offers do not carry the wrong guarantee; they carry none. Any honest guarantee usually beats silence, and the right one beats the rest.
