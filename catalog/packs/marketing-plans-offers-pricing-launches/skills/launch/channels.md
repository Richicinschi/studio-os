# Owned, rented and borrowed channels

Rule of thumb: attention can come from anywhere, but it should end up somewhere you control.

## Owned

You control the channel itself, though people can still unsubscribe or leave. No algorithm sits between you and the reader.

Examples: email list, blog, podcast, a community space you run, your site and product.

Why they pay off: they get stronger the longer you run them, the platform cannot change the rules on you, you talk to people directly, and old content keeps working.

Choose one or two to start, based on the gap you see:
- Few good articles exist in your field: start a blog.
- Your audience wants steady news: build the email list first.
- People want to talk to each other: open a community.

Illustration (invented): a small budgeting app keeps its beta closed and invite-only. Each person accepted gets a 20-minute screen-share setup call with the founder. Invitations feel scarce, people tell friends, and the founder learns directly what confuses newcomers. All of it runs through channels the founder owns.

## Rented

Platforms that give reach but not control: social networks, app stores and marketplaces, video sites, discussion forums. Reach can vanish with an algorithm change or a new fee.

Use them well:
- Pick one or two where your audience already spends time.
- Treat them as a way to bring people to your owned channels.
- Never make them the whole plan.

Platform moves:
- Short-post networks: a thread that starts a conversation, ending with a pointer to your newsletter.
- Professional networks: a useful post that leads to a gated guide or an email signup.
- Marketplaces and plug-in stores: a sharp listing whose main job is to send people to your site.

Illustration (invented): a note-taking tool's users share templates on video and forum sites. The company welcomes it, but every shared template links to a signup page, and new signups enter a targeted email sequence. Rented reach gives speed; the signup is what keeps it.

## Borrowed

Someone else's audience, used to skip the slowest part: being noticed.

Examples: guest articles, podcast interviews, newsletter features; joint webinars and co-marketing; conference, panel or summit talks; influencer partnerships.

Be active:
1. Write down the people and shows your audience already follows.
2. Pitch something that benefits both sides.
3. Use audience-overlap research tools to find partners you would not think of.
4. Offer affiliate or referral terms. For partner-led launches, a deal-registration tool can track who brought which customer and what commission is owed.

Illustration (invented): a maker of a pocket weather display mails a free unit to a hobbyist reviewer with no strings attached. The reviewer likes it, posts a long video, and a measurable share of that audience buys. An affiliate link in the description keeps paying afterwards. Note that gifting a product and offering commission are spending decisions: ask the owner first.

Borrowed attention fades unless you turn it into your own list or accounts, so every borrowed placement needs a landing page with a capture step.
