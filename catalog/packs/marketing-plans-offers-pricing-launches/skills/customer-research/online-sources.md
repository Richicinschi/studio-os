# Gathering from public sources

Public posts are unmoderated and show real wording. Quote only what is public, keep the link and date, and do not collect handles or personal details. Do not log in to private groups or bypass any site rule.

## Choosing sources by audience
- Business software buyers: role-specific forums, review sites, a tech news forum, professional network posts.
- Small business and founders: entrepreneur communities, maker forums, launch sites, social groups.
- Developers and ops: language and devops forums, Q&A sites, chat servers.
- Consumers: app store and shop reviews (1 to 3 stars first), hobby forums, video and short-clip comments.
- Enterprise: professional network, analyst write-ups, enterprise-tier review filters, job ads.

Shortcuts: know the category, start with review sites for you and rivals. Want to know where the audience hangs out, use an audience-intelligence tool. Want raw wording, use forums and video comments. Want trigger events, use professional posts, job ads and "Ask" threads. Want competitor intel, read their 4-star reviews and launch-site discussions.

## Forum communities
Find where your buyer talks about the problem, not where your product is named. Ways in: a web search limited to the forum host plus a role or problem phrase; the forum's own community search; communities that show up when you search the buyer's problems; communities named in rivals' reviews.

Search shapes (replace the parts in brackets):
- host-limited search for "[problem]" with "recommend" OR "alternative"
- host-limited search for "[rival]" with "vs" OR "switched"
- inside one community, "[keyword]"

Valuable post kinds: "what do you use for X" (alternatives, vocabulary), "fed up with [rival]" (pain, switching trigger), "how do you handle X" (workarounds), "is [category] worth it" (objections, criteria), rival complaint threads (gaps). Note the problem as stated, the top-voted advice, complaints in replies, exact wording, and whether votes show agreement or a fight. An archive search service helps with old threads.

## Review sites
Your own reviews, in this order: 3 stars (honest middle), 1 star (failure modes; split product problems from support or onboarding), 5 stars (what they love, your proof), 4 stars (the buried "only thing I wish"). Note what they use it for, what is hardest, what they compare it to, role and industry signals.

Rival 4-star reviews are the richest: the reviewer likes it and still complains. Use the site's fields: "best" gives their strengths, "dislike" gives your openings, "problems solved" gives the job. Other sites: a second business-software site with the same layout, a consumer review site, and a deal-site for small-business tools.

Per rival review, log: job, top praise, top complaint, switching context, unmet need.

## Launch sites and founder forums
Founder forums: question posts, stack descriptions that reveal tool taste and pain, threads under category launches. Launch sites: read discussion tabs of rivals. Questions are pre-sale worries, comments are early reactions, "alternatives to X" lists show the market as users see it.

## Technical news forum
Skews to builders and doubters. Useful: "Ask" threads on best tools, skeptical comments under rival launch posts. Expect critique of architecture, business model and especially subscription pricing.

## Professional network
Posts: people describing workflows, "we used to X, now Y" stories, tool-request posts (replies come from active buyers). Job ads show pain: required versus nice-to-have tools, the outcomes named, what the role spends its time on.

## Video comments
Pick tutorials for the problem, "best tools for X" roundups, rival demos. Look for "does this work for [case]" (edge cases, unmet needs), "I tried this but" (failure), "what about [rival]" (active comparison), timestamped questions (confusing steps). For consumers add "honest review" and "after six months" videos.

## Short posts on a social feed
Search for live complaints about rivals and practitioners sharing their stack, e.g. rival name with a minimum-likes filter, or "[category] is broken". Also note whom the audience follows, for distribution later.

## Comparison content and niche communities
Search "[rival] vs [rival]" and "best [category] software". Comment sections hold questions your sales process must answer. Niche rooms: industry chat groups, server communities, social groups (strong for small shops, agencies, coaches), paid member communities.

## Consumer products
Buyers leave traces in stores and social posts, not on professional networks.
- App store reviews: 1 to 2 stars (broken expectations), 3 stars (trade-offs), 5 stars (their words for value). Extract the job ("I use this to"), the moment it failed, what they switched from, emotional wording. Sort newest for fresh signal, then most critical for pains.
- Shop reviews: same order, 3 stars first.
- Hobby forums: go vertical (for a running app, a running forum, not a general one).
- Short-video and photo-feed comments: search "[product] review" or "is [product] worth it", watch the top handful and read all comments. Questions mean unmet needs or fuzzy positioning; "I switched from X" means a switching trigger; price and broken-promise complaints matter.
- Other: neighbourhood boards for local services, long-form Q&A sites for decision anxiety.

## Audience-intelligence tools
These aggregate behaviour at scale: sites read, podcasts, channels, forums, searches, accounts followed, demographics. Use one first to learn where the audience goes, then mine those places by hand for wording. Run queries by rival audience, by topic talkers, by role in bio, and on your own site visitors to compare. Treat behaviour data as medium to high confidence, demographics as medium, and never as a stand-in for qualitative evidence. Limits to check before relying on it: free tier caps, no programmatic access, English and US bias, shows what and not why. Paid plans cost money, so ask before subscribing.

## Tagging and the running log
Tags: #pain, #trigger, #outcome, #language, #alternative, #objection, #competitor. Log columns: source, date, quote, tags, notes. Patterns show once the log has enough rows for the same wording to repeat; keep logging until new rows stop adding new themes. Wording that appears in several unrelated sources is the strongest finding.
