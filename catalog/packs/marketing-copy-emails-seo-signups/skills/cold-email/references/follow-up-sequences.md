# Follow-up sequences

Many replies arrive after a follow-up, yet many senders never send one, so a sequence is where much of the result comes from. Check this in your own data: count how many replies came after the second touch or later.

## Length

- Longer sequences can bring more replies, but each extra step also raises the risk of complaints. Measure both by step in your own sends, and stop adding steps once later steps bring few replies or more complaints.
- Working rule (a starting point, not a measured value): the first mail plus at most 4 follow-ups, and each one must bring something new.

## Cadence

The days below are the skill's own starting point; tune them against your replies.

| Touch | Day | Role |
|---|---|---|
| First mail | 0 | Your best-researched message |
| Follow-up 1 | 3 | A second angle |
| Follow-up 2 | 9 | Proof from a similar company |
| Follow-up 3 | 16 | An insight or useful resource |
| Follow-up 4 | 25 to 30 | Closing note |

A starting point for send times: Tuesday to Thursday, 10 to noon or 2 to 4 in the afternoon, in the reader's time zone, skipping Monday mornings and Friday afternoons. Test it on your own list.

## One new idea per mail

Each step carries exactly one new reason to care: a statistic, a customer story, a trend, a resource. Every step should read fine to someone who never saw the earlier ones. A scenario to model on, for a payroll tool aimed at a restaurant group:

- Step 1: their new location opening means a second payroll run to manage.
- Step 2: a similar group cut month-end payroll from two days to half a day.
- Step 3: a short checklist on tracking tips across locations.
- Step 4: the closing note.

## The closing note

It removes pressure, which is why it can get the best reply rate of the whole sequence. Check your own per-step reply counts to see whether it does. Four parts: say you have written a few times, accept that the timing may be wrong, say this is the last message for now, leave the door open.

Sample wording: "No reply so far, so I will stop here. One last thing that might help: the checklist above works even if you never talk to us. If payroll becomes a headache later, you know where I am."

A low-friction variant asks for a single digit: 1 for interested, 2 for ask again in a quarter, 3 for do not contact. Whatever form you use, keep the promise. Once sent, that contact gets no further cold mail, and a "3" goes on the suppression list at once.

## Phrases that cost replies

- "I never heard back"
- "Just checking in" or "bumping this up"
- "Did you see my last email?"
- "Following up on my previous message"

All of them add nothing and make the reader feel chased.

## Ask size by seniority

Senior readers get the smallest possible ask ("Worth a look?"). Managers can take a specific offer ("Want me to show how a group your size saved 12 hours a week?"). The higher the title, the less effort you can request.
