# Influence principles

Persuasion helps a buyer reach a choice they would still endorse later. The test for each item below: would the buyer feel respected if they saw how it works?

- **Reciprocity.** Give something useful first: a checklist, a calculator, a generous free plan. Do not attach hidden strings; the gift must be worth having on its own.
- **Commitment and consistency.** A small yes makes a bigger yes more likely. Start with a newsletter sign-up or a sample, then offer the paid step. Never trap people by making the small step secretly binding.
- **Authority.** People trust credible expertise. Show real qualifications, certifications, press that actually covered you, and expert reviews. Do not display badges you did not earn.
- **Liking and similarity.** We say yes to people like us. A founder story, relatable customers and plain community language help a buyer see themselves.
- **Unity.** Shared identity is a strong bond: "built by bakers for bakers". Use it only if it is true, and keep the language of the group natural.
- **Scarcity and urgency.** Limited supply raises perceived value. Use it only when the limit is real: 40 print copies, a cohort capped at 15 seats, an event on a fixed date. No fake countdown timers, no invented "only 2 left", no deadlines that quietly reset.
- **Foot in the door.** Move in steps: free trial, monthly plan, yearly plan, team plan. Each step must deliver value on its own.
- **Door in the face.** A large first ask makes the smaller one look moderate. In pricing, this means showing the full-service package next to the starter plan so the buyer sees the range. Both offers must be real and the larger one must not be a prop.
- **Loss aversion.** A loss tends to weigh more than an equal gain. Phrase benefits as protection when it is accurate: "keep your 3 years of photos safe" beats "get backups". Avoid scare tactics or exaggerated risks.
- **Anchoring.** The first number seen shapes later judgement. Show a true reference price: the standard rate, the competitor's published price, the cost of the problem. A "was" price that was never charged is deception.
- **Decoy effect.** A third option can steer a choice between the other two. Use it only so the buyer compares clearly; each tier must be a plausible pick for someone, never a throwaway designed to mislead.
- **Framing.** The same fact lands differently: "9 in 10 customers renew" versus "1 in 10 leaves". Choose the accurate frame that helps, and never hide the other side where it matters (side effects, fees, limits).
- **Contrast.** Differences are noticed side by side. Show the before and after, the old process next to the new one, with real measurements.
