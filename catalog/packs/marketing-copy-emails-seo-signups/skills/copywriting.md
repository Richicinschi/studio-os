# Page copywriting

## When to use

Use it for website text that has to persuade or convert: a homepage, landing page, pricing page, feature page, about page or product page. Typical wordings: "write copy for my landing page", "this hero is weak", "give me a tagline", "help me describe my product", "make this more compelling", "rewrite this page", "headline help", "CTA copy", "value proposition", "subheadline", "above the fold", "marketing copy".

Neighbouring skills take over here:
- Email copy: emails.
- Popup or modal copy: popups.
- Line-by-line polish of text that already exists: copy-editing (also the last step after your own draft).
- The offer under the copy (bonuses, guarantee, value framing): offers.
- Page structure or strategy is the real problem, not the words: cro.
- Testing two versions of copy: ab-testing.

## How it works

1. **Look for saved context.** If a product marketing context file exists in the project (under `.agents/`, under `.claude/`, or with the older long file name), read it first and ask only for what it lacks.
2. **Get the brief.** Ask for what is missing:
   - Page: which type, and the one action a visitor should take.
   - Audience: who the ideal customer is, the problem, their doubts, and the words they use for it.
   - Offer: what is sold, how it differs from alternatives, the change or result it delivers, and any real proof (numbers, testimonials, case studies). Use only proof the user supplies.
   - Arrival: where traffic comes from (ads, organic, email) and what visitors already know.
3. **Fix the voice.** Pick a formality level (casual, friendly-professional, formal) and a personality (playful or serious, bold or quiet, technical or plain). Keep it consistent, but let headlines be bolder, body text plainer and buttons action-first.
4. **Draft by the principles and structure below.** Use [headline formulas](copywriting/references/headlines.md), [sections and templates](copywriting/references/page-sections.md), and [transition phrases](copywriting/references/transitions.md).
5. **Deliver in this shape:**
   - The copy, section by section: headline, subheadline, button, section titles and body, secondary buttons.
   - A short note per key choice: why, and which principle it applies.
   - Two or three options for each headline and button, each with a one-line reason.
   - If it matters for the page: the title tag and meta description.
6. **Hand off.** Run a copy-editing pass on the draft for a line-level review.

### Principles

- Clear beats clever. When the two conflict, pick clear.
- Benefits over features. A feature is what the product does; a benefit is what changes for the customer.
- Specific beats vague. "Work smarter every day" is weak; a figure the user can confirm, such as minutes per task before and after, is strong. Never make up the figure.
- Use the customer's words, taken from reviews, interviews and support tickets.
- One idea per section, in an order that builds an argument down the page.

### Style rules

- Plain words: "use", not "utilize".
- Skip empty words like "streamline", "optimize", "innovative".
- Active voice: "We generate reports", not "Reports are generated".
- Be confident: cut "almost", "very", "really".
- Show the outcome rather than propping a verb up with an adverb.
- Be honest. Invented numbers or testimonials destroy trust and carry legal risk.
- Say the point first. Compare "Our platform enables the seamless exchange of booking information among team members" (buried) with "Moving a booking? Tell the whole crew in one tap" (direct).
- A question can pull a reader in ("Still waiting on a signature?"); an analogy can make an abstract idea concrete; a joke is fine only if it suits the brand and does not blur the meaning.

### Page structure

Above the fold:
- The headline carries the single most important message and the core value, and is specific rather than generic. Starting shapes: achieve the outcome without the pain; the category for the audience; never suffer the annoying thing again; a question that names the main pain.
- The subheadline widens the headline with detail, in at most two sentences.
- The main button names what the visitor gets: "Start free trial" beats "Sign up".

Body, in rough order:

| Section | Job |
|---|---|
| Social proof | Credibility: logos, figures, testimonials (real ones only) |
| Problem | Show you understand their situation |
| Solution | Link to outcomes, 3 to 5 benefits |
| How it works | Lower the felt effort, 3 to 4 steps |
| Objections | FAQ, comparison, guarantee |
| Final call | Recap the value, repeat the button, remove risk |

### Buttons

Avoid: Submit, Sign Up, Learn More, Click Here, Get Started. Prefer: Start Free Trial, Get the Template Pack, Watch a 2-Minute Demo, Build Your First Schedule, Read the Setup Guide. Formula: action verb, then what they get, then a qualifier if needed ("Start My Free Trial", "Get Plans for My Studio").

### By page type

- Homepage: speak to several audiences without going generic; lead with the broadest value; give each visitor intent a clear path.
- Landing page: one message and one button; the headline mirrors the ad or source; the whole argument fits on the page.
- Pricing page: help people choose, calm the "which plan is for me?" worry, and make the recommended plan obvious.
- Feature page: walk feature, then benefit, then outcome; add use cases; show a clear route to try or buy.
- About page: tell why you exist, tie the mission to what the customer gains, and still end with a button.

## Checks before done

- Every figure, quote and logo came from the user or a source they gave; none is invented.
- No jargon an outsider would trip on, no sentence carrying two jobs, no passive constructions, no exclamation marks, no buzzwords without substance.
- One primary action for the page, and the button text says what happens.
- Headline and button have alternatives with reasons.
- English only; no em or en dashes in the output.
